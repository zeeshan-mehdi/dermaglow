Niacinamide (vitamin B3) is one of the few skincare ingredients that is both genuinely well-studied and well-tolerated by most skin types. It also gets oversold. Here's the honest version.

## What the evidence supports

Niacinamide has reasonable clinical backing for a handful of specific effects:

- **Barrier support.** It boosts production of ceramides and free fatty acids, the lipids that hold your outer skin layer together. Better barrier means less water loss and less reactivity.
- **Oil regulation.** Studies show reduced sebum output over several weeks of use, which is why it turns up in so many products aimed at oily and combination skin.
- **Pigmentation.** Niacinamide interferes with the transfer of pigment from melanocytes to surface skin cells. It won't erase melasma, but it meaningfully softens post-inflammatory marks — the brown or red patches left behind after a spot heals.
- **Redness.** Its anti-inflammatory action helps calm general blotchiness.

## The concentration question

More is not better. Most of the clinical work sits in the **2–5%** range, and that's where the benefit-to-irritation ratio is best.

The 10% products you see marketed aren't dramatically more effective — they're just more likely to cause stinging, flushing or a bumpy reaction in sensitive skin. If you're new to niacinamide, start around 4–5%.

## How to fit it into a routine

Niacinamide is unusually easy to place. It's stable, it doesn't require a particular pH, and it plays well with almost everything.

1. Cleanse.
2. Apply niacinamide to damp skin.
3. Moisturiser.
4. SPF in the morning.

> The old warning that niacinamide "cancels out" vitamin C came from research on raw ingredients under heat, not on finished formulas. In modern products, using both is fine. If you get flushing, separate them — one in the morning, one at night.

## Who should be careful

If your skin is reactive or you have rosacea, introduce it slowly — every other day for two weeks. A small number of people flush from niacinamide regardless of concentration; if that's you, it simply isn't your ingredient, and that's fine.

## What to expect, and when

Oil control tends to show up first, around **weeks 2–4**. Barrier improvement and reduced redness follow around **weeks 4–8**. Pigmentation changes are slowest — give it a full **8–12 weeks** before judging.

Take a photo at the start. Skin changes slowly enough that memory is a genuinely unreliable narrator.
